\title{Stable repetition produces habits in multiattribute choices without feedback}
\shorttitle{Habits without Feedback}
\authorsnames[1,{2,3},1]{Omar David Perez, Bastian Henriquez-Jara, Sebastian Mu\~{n}oz}
\authorsaffiliations{{Department of Industrial Engineering, Universidad de Chile, Santiago, Chile},
{Department of Civil Engineering, Universidad de Chile, Santiago, Chile},
{Complex Engineering Systems Institute (ISCI), Santiago, Chile}}
\leftheader{Perez et al.}

\abstract{When do repeated actions become habits? Environmental stability appears critical: when a decision is repeated in the same context, its features can retrieve the decision without consideration of its current consequences, employing little attention to the relevant options' attributes. We tested 114 adults using an overtrained discrete-choice task without outcome feedback. Participants made 150 choices among options whose attributes changed across trials; after 100 trials, the stable spatial layout was disrupted. Consistent with the animal-learning definition of habit, participants increasingly repeated previous choices that were no longer best under their current utility, despite stable underlying preferences. This value-insensitive responding was accompanied by narrower, more stereotyped attention. Disrupting the layout broadened attention and restored value-sensitive choice: participants again repeated previous choices mainly when current utility supported them. These results show that repetition in a stable decision environment can produce choice habits without feedback and identify attention as a key variable in its formation.}

\keywords{attention, discrete choice, habits, inertia, eye tracking}

Consider how you decide to travel to work after moving to a new city. On the first few occasions you compare the available modes---bus, metro, ride-hailing---on the attributes that matter to you: what each costs, how long it takes, and how comfortable it is. You pay attention to each attribute and weigh it against your preferences. Then you choose. A decision made this way is usually described as goal-directed, or value based, because it is selected by considering its expected consequences. 

With repetition in a stable environment, however, after you have already learned the best option according to your preferences, the action strategy changes: you no longer inspect and weigh each attribute each morning; the situation itself retrieves the choice you made before. The action has become a habit. In habit mode, features of the environment make you repeat a decision without requiring its consequences to be reconsidered each time \citep{wood2002,buyalskaya2023can}.

This dual-system view of behavior has a direct consequence. If the value of the options changes while the environment remains familiar---a fare rises, a line becomes slower---the habit can produce a mistake. You may continue to take the same mode when another has become better, because the familiar situation retrieves the previous choice without its current consequences being reconsidered. This is what defines a habit: repetition against your own current preferences. 

Imagine now that the choice environment becomes unpredictable---the routes are rearranged, or the information is presented differently. In this case, the previous choice can no longer be retrieved so easily, and the attributes must be re-attended and compared again. Habits are therefore learned and expressed in stable environments, but uncertainty makes the past less informative and creates a reason to inspect the present and behave in a goal-directed manner, considering the attributes and the current utility that each option carries. Recent animal evidence supports this idea: habits form mostly in stable environments \citep{thrailkill2018} and unexpected changes can return those habits to goal-directed, value-based control \citep{bouton2020,bouton2024}. The same idea has been put forward in computational reinforcement learning theories, which assign behavioral control to whichever system's predictions are more reliable, so that stability should favor repeating a habit whereas disruption should restore sensitivity to current information \citep{daw2005,lee2014}.

In experimental settings, the cardinal test of a habit is the outcome revaluation procedure. In these experiments, subjects are first left to perform an action in a stable context for a valuable outcome until they reliably repeat the decision (pressing a lever in the case of rats; buttons or other manipulanda in humans). Then, the outcome is revalued by pairing it with an aversive event or by satiating subjects with it. The test consists of presenting the previously rewarded manipulandum but in the absence of further outcome presentations (so that no new learning with the revalued outcome can occur). If subjects tend to repeat the action despite it not currently being valued, the action is considered a habit \citep{adamsDickinson1981,dickinson1985,perezDickinson2020}. An important aspect of this procedure is that it ensures that subjects reject the outcome when it is offered for free before testing if they repeat the previously-valued action. Its new, lower value is therefore represented, but it no longer controls the decision. Just as in the commuting example, repetition against one's own current preferences is the critical feature of an animal habit.   

So far, the idea that choice repetition in a stable context leads to habit formation has not been demonstrated in humans. With the exception of a single fMRI study \citep{tricomi2009}, attempts to produce habits using extended training in stable environments have been largely unsuccessful.  This point was first raised by  \cite{deWit2018}, who across  several experiments found no effect of extended training on sensitivity to outcome devaluation. A subsequent preregistered multilaboratory investigation that attempted to replicate \citet{tricomi2009} also failed to find the effect \citep{pool2022}. These failures, of course, do not imply that human behavior cannot become habitual---we know it does---but show that repetition in stable environments, as typically arranged in human experiments, has not been sufficient to produce the change in value control that defines a habit.

Attention may provide the process through which the goal-to-habit transition occurs. A predictable environment allows attention to narrow around the attributes that have proved decisive; a change requires the information to be located again, so that the new best option is identified through a fresh comparison of attributes. Recent discoveries in animals link habits to declining attention to action consequences and their disruption to the recapture of attention \citep{thrailkill2018,bouton2020,bouton2024}. Attentional narrowing should accompany reduced control by current value, whereas environmental disruption should broaden attention and restore value-sensitive behavior.

Discrete-choice experiments (DCEs), popular in Economics, Marketing and Transportation to assess people's preferences for different hypothetical options, provide an unusual test of this prediction \citep[see ][]{louviere2000stated,hensher2015applied}. In these experiments, participants repeatedly choose among alternatives described by attributes such as cost, time, comfort, and environmental impact. These attributes change across trials and determine the current utility of each alternative. Because participants have different underlying preferences for each attribute, the utility of the options is different across them. For example, a person may value more travel time than comfort, but another one may assign more importance to cost than the rest of attributes. Standard models treat the resulting choices as expressions of stable preferences for each attribute to recover relative values such as willingness-to-pay (how much a participant is willing to trade changes in one attribute, for example price, for another, like comfort) \citep{mcfadden1974,train2009}.  

DCEs have demonstrated an impressive applicability to real-world problems. Their analysis has allowed us to focus and direct resources to particular market demands, increasing social and economic welfare. Importantly, they allow researchers to analyze preferences for alternatives that do not necessarily exist and for non-market goods---for example, to estimate willingness to pay for a decrease in environmental pollution---where revealed-preference data cannot be collected. 

Being mostly economic-based, however, such analyzes rely on the assumption that people compare the option's attributes in a rational manner, using all the available information to maximize their expected utility. More psychologically-inspired analyzes, on the other hand, have questioned these simplifying assumptions and asked whether participants inspect all available information or use different strategies and heuristics instead of fully sampling the options and attributes \citep{hole2011,yegoryan2020,tversky1969}.

%A non-obvious question is whether choices obtained through DCEs are reliable. Responses could be completely random, since individuals are not necessarily affected by the choices they make. The design of the experiment is key for obtaining reliable responses. High-complexity choice tasks increase the variability of responses \citep{caussade2005assessing} or induce respondents to rely on heuristics \citep{swait2001choice}. 

Critically, the number of trials presented to participants in DCEs is usually kept low. The main concern among researchers in the area is that making too many choices in a DCE may generate more random responding, likely due to fatigue \citep{Bradley1994}, or else, after learning  their own preferences, people may start making more deterministic choices, both of which affect the reliability of the modelling estimations. In spite of this, the literature suggests that neither learning nor fatigue effects are the predominant behavior \citep{campbell2015learning,hess2012not,czajkowski2014learning}.

Previous studies addressing preference evolution in DCEs have analyzed changes in the scale parameter of the random utility model (RUM)---or how exploratory versus exploitative choices are---yielding mixed results \citep{hess2012not}. However, drawing on the psychology literature, a formal test is needed to assess the emergence of habitual behavior, which cannot be established solely by analyzing increases or decreases in the stochasticity of choices.

In this paper, we claim that DCEs provide a testbed for human habitual behavior. Incorporating the methods and definition of habit from psychology, we claim that extended training in a stable DCE environment may encourage deployment of less attentional resources and more habits. To this end, we adapt the value criterion of habits into repeated multiattribute choices. By construction, in a DCE the previously selected alternative can remain the best under current attribute values or else cease to be the best because its attributes are changing across trials. In the first case, staying with the same option is supported by current value. By contrast, staying in the second case is precisely the habit definition: the previous response persists even though current value supports switching. Under this approach, habit formation should be evident as an increase in value-inconsistent staying, just as in the case of animals repeating choices that are no longer desirable. Making the environment unstable should recover attention and make habits less likely, that is, the probability of staying with the same option against current preferences should decrease.  

Importantly, in contrast to previous experiments, DCEs provide no realized outcome and no reward feedback. If participants  increasingly behave habitually by repeating a previous choice when its current attributes no longer support it, the effect cannot be attributed to reinforcement from obtained outcomes. This possibility follows an old idea. Guthrie proposed that contiguity between a situation and a response can strengthen behavior without reward \citep{guthrie1935}; more recent computational work has revived this possibility \citep{millerHabits2019,nebe2024,thorndike1911edward}. The present study therefore contains another novel prediction: habits can arise from freely generated, value-based choices in humans making repeated choices that provide no explicit feedback.

We tested these predictions in a long 150-trial DCE. The visual environment remained stable for 100 trials while gaze was recorded. At trial 101, only the spatial positions of the attribute rows changed; the alternatives and their values did not. We predicted that overtraining in the stable decision environment would narrow attention and increase value-inconsistent staying while relative preferences remained stable---habits would form under those circumstances. In addition, we expected that disrupting the learned environment would broaden attention and restore value-sensitive choice. By merging the two research traditions, we test if extended choice repetition in a stable choice environment can produce habitual choice in humans in the absence of outcome feedback.

\section{Method}

We report how we determined our sample sizes, all data exclusions, all manipulations, and all measures collected in both studies. Neither the laboratory study nor the online study was preregistered. AI assistance (Anthropic Claude) was used to assist with language editing for non-native authors; it was not used to generate, analyze, or interpret data, and all scientific claims, analyzes, and final text were determined and verified by the authors.

\subsection{Participants}

\subsubsection{Laboratory sample.} Forty-five adults were recruited through internal university networks and an announcement posted on the institutional portal of the Faculty of Physical and Mathematical Sciences, Universidad de Chile. Eligibility was open to adults aged 18 to 75 years. Participants were tested individually in the NeuroLab facility of the Department of Industrial Engineering. Thirty-two received a fixed payment of around \$10 USD that did not depend on the options they chose; the remaining 13 participants took part for course credit through an interdisciplinary module.

Of the 45 participants recruited, 18 were women and 27 were men, and 15 wore corrective lenses during the session. Ages ranged 18--30($M = 21.5$, $SD = 2.7$, range 18--30). Most were members of the Universidad de Chile community. Before starting, participants rated their current energy level on a five-point scale ($M = 3.4$, $SD = 1.1$, $n = 40$); this measure was collected for monitoring purposes and is not analyzed here.

\subsubsection{Online sample.} Seventy-eight additional adults were recruited through Prolific and completed the same 150-trial task in the browser, without eye tracking. The online version was implemented in Pavlovia. Sessions that were abandoned before completion, or whose submission was returned or timed out on the recruitment platform, generated partial data files but were not counted as participants and were not analyzed.

The online sample differed markedly from the laboratory sample in age and background. Participants averaged 37.1 years of age ($SD = 13.4$, range 18--76); 41 were women and 37 were men. Fifty-one resided in the United Kingdom and 27 in the United States, and only 12 were students, the remainder being in full-time work, part-time work, or not in paid employment. Compensation was a fixed payment at a rate of approximately \pounds10 per hour, set through the recruitment platform, and did not depend on the options chosen.
Participants were assigned in equal numbers to the two scrambling schedules, 39 to each. The median session lasted 36.4~min.

\subsubsection{Exclusions.} The same three criteria were applied to both samples before any analysis: a participant was excluded if the whole task lasted longer than 1~hr, if any single trial lasted longer than 3~min, or if the same alternative was selected on more than 80\% of trials. The first two criteria identify interrupted or unattended sessions; the third identifies participants who did not engage with the attribute information at all. Thresholds were set after inspecting the distributions of task duration and response variability, and before the habit analyzes reported here. These criteria excluded 3 laboratory participants and 6 online participants, leaving 42 and 72 participants respectively, for a total of 114.

\subsubsection{Sample size.} No a priori power analysis was conducted. The laboratory sample size was determined by previous DCE studies involving habit-like attention (N= 40; see \cite{pool2019}) and eye-tracking . The online sample was sized to provide a substantially larger set of choice observations for the joint estimation. Because the design places 150 observations within each participant, the number of trials rather than the number of participants is the binding constraint on the trial-level analyzes. % TODO: confirm this description matches how collection actually stopped, and add the recruitment window dates.

\subsubsection{Ethics.} The protocol was approved by the Comit\'{e} de \'{E}tica of the Faculty of Physical and Mathematical Sciences, Universidad de Chile (Protocol No. ID 15938). All participants provided written informed consent before taking part.

\subsection{Design}

Both studies used a within-participant design with 150 choice trials divided into two consecutive phases. During the \emph{stable phase} (trials 1--100), the spatial arrangement of the choice display was held completely constant. During the \emph{disrupted phase} (trials 101--150), the vertical order of the attribute rows was permuted, beginning with an unannounced permutation at trial 101.

The row order presented during the stable phase was randomized across participants and then held fixed for all 100 training trials. The horizontal order of the three alternatives was identical for all participants and never changed. Any effect of the disruption is therefore not attributable to one particular layout, and the manipulation acted on the attribute dimension only, leaving the identity and screen position of each alternative intact.

The two studies differed only in the schedule of permutations during the disrupted phase. In the laboratory study, rows were permuted every five trials. In the online study, participants were assigned in equal numbers to a fixed-interval condition, which used the same five-trial schedule, or to a variable-interval condition, in which the number of trials between successive permutations was drawn from a Uniform(1, 10) distribution. Response-time trajectories and post-disruption recovery did not differ significantly between the two online conditions, so they were combined for all analyzes reported below.

Critically, the manipulation changed only where information appeared. The three alternatives, the four attributes, the distribution from which attribute values were drawn, and the economic structure of the decision were identical across phases.

\subsection{Materials and apparatus}

On each trial, participants chose among three commuting alternatives---bus, metro, and ride-hailing---presented as the columns of a $4 \times 3$ matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:interface}). The four rows were the attributes: comfort (0--5 stars), cost (US\$), pollution (expressed as a number of trees, representing CO\textsubscript{2} emissions), and travel time (min). Alternative labels appeared in a header row above the matrix; attribute labels, with their units, appeared in a label column to the left of the matrix. Each attribute row was rendered in a distinct background color, so that a row could be identified by its color as well as by its position, and the color moved with the row when the layout was permuted. All attribute values were integers and were displayed in white boxes at the intersection of the corresponding row and column.

Attribute levels were alternative specific, so that each mode spanned a plausible range for that mode (Table~\ref{tab:levels}). Choice sets were built by combining these levels and then optimizing the design with the ChoiceDesign Python package \citep{ignacio2020choicedesign}, which reduces the variance among presented values and minimizes dominated alternatives. No alternative was better than both others on every attribute, so each trial required a tradeoff rather than a lookup. Values were drawn so that a zero appeared with probability below 0.035\%.

The laboratory task was implemented in PsychoPy and run on three Acer TravelMate P215-54 laptops with external monitors. Gaze was recorded with Tobii Pro Spark eye trackers sampling at 60~Hz. Room lighting was controlled so that it did not interfere with the participant's view of the screen.

\subsection{Procedure}

After providing written consent, participants were seated and given the task instructions. They were asked to place themselves in a scenario in which they had to get to work, and to choose the option they most preferred on each trial. They were also asked not to move their head or chair during calibration and testing, to keep their gaze on the screen, not to cover their face, to avoid interruptions such as retrieving items or answering the phone, and not to discuss the experiment with peers. The eye tracker was then calibrated using the manufacturer's routine, and the calibration was repeated until it was judged acceptable (median 1 attempt, range 1--5). For five participants the best achievable calibration remained slightly weak in the upper region of the display. Participants then saw a short instruction screen showing where to click.

Participants then completed the 150 trials without a break. Trials were self-paced: the display remained on screen until the participant clicked one of the three alternatives, and the next trial began immediately. Participants were not told that the task had phases, and they received no warning that the layout would change.

No outcome was ever realized and no feedback of any kind was delivered. Participants were not told whether a choice was good, no commute was simulated or experienced, and payment did not depend on the options chosen. The values shown on the screen were therefore the only information available about the worth of each alternative on a given trial, which is what makes it possible to attribute any growth in repetition to the stability of the display rather than to reinforcement from obtained outcomes.The online study followed the same procedure, delivered in the browser without eye tracking or calibration. 

After the task, participants completed a short questionnaire recording age, gender, and ocular conditions, and rated how relevant each of the four attributes had been to their decisions. Participants also described their experience of the task. These reports establish that the disruption was consciously detected: participants reported noticing the change and being surprised by it, and identified the row colors as the cue, because the colors moved with the rows when the order was permuted. The manipulation was therefore salient rather than subliminal, which is what the attention-recapture account requires; we return to this point in the Discussion.

\subsection{Eye tracking and gaze measures}

The choice screen was divided into 19 non-overlapping regions of interest (ROIs): the 12 cells holding the attribute values (4 attributes $\times$ 3 alternatives), the 4 attribute labels, and the 3 alternative labels (Figure~\ref{fig:interface}). Dwell time was computed for each ROI on each trial by summing the durations of all gaze samples falling within it. These dwell times supplied both the attribute-specific measures and the trial-level gaze entropy defined in Equations~\ref{eq:shannon_entropy} and following.

The following were recorded on every trial: participant identifier, trial number, response time, chosen alternative, the attribute values presented for each alternative, the screen coordinates of every label and value, dwell time per ROI, and pupil diameter per frame. Pupil diameter was recorded but is not analyzed here.

A small number of trials were recorded with invalid (negative) durations, the result of a timing error in the presentation script that was corrected during data collection; these trials were removed. All remaining trials were retained for analysis.

\subsection{Operationalizing habitual control}

We first estimated an attribute-only choice model that excluded previous-choice inertia. For each participant and trial, this model provided the systematic utility of every alternative from its current attributes. Let $c_{n,t-1}$ denote participant $n$'s previous choice. We classified that alternative as currently best when

\begin{equation}
V_{n,t,c_{n,t-1}}=\max_j V_{n,t,j},
\end{equation}

and as not best otherwise. A stay occurred when $c_{n,t}=c_{n,t-1}$. Habitual control was operationalized as staying when the previous alternative was no longer supported by current utility:

\begin{equation}
P(c_{n,t}=c_{n,t-1}\mid
V_{n,t,c_{n,t-1}}<\max_j V_{n,t,j}).
\end{equation}

For comparison, we also calculated staying when the previous alternative remained best. We compared these conditional probabilities during early training (trials 1--10), late training (trials 91--100), and immediately after the layout disruption (trials 101--110). Because inertia was excluded when current utility was calculated, prior choice could not make an alternative count as best. All inferential models preserved the panel structure of repeated trials within participants.

\subsection{Latent-attention choice model}

To test whether attention changes the balance between current attribute information and previous responses, we modeled utility as a function of the attributes and a trial-specific inertia term:

\begin{equation}
U_{n,t,i} =
V(X_{n,t,i}, \beta_{n,t})
+ \beta_{\text{Inertia},n,t} I_{n,t,i}
+ \varepsilon_{n,t,i}.
\end{equation}

Here, $I_{n,t,i}$ equals 1 when alternative $i$ was chosen on the preceding trial. The attribute and inertia coefficients were allowed to vary with latent attention and to have separate trajectories before and after the layout disruption:

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \beta_{k,n,t} =&\beta_{k0}^{\mathrm{pre}}D^{\mathrm{pre}}_{nt} +\beta_{k0}^{\mathrm{post}}D^{\mathrm{post}}_{nt} + \biggl( \beta_{k,\text{LatAttention}}^{\mathrm{pre}}D^{\mathrm{pre}}_{nt} \\
&+\beta_{k,\text{LatAttention}}^{\mathrm{post}}D^{\mathrm{post}}_{nt} \biggr)\text{LatAttention}_{n,t}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}

Attention was modeled as a latent process measured through gaze entropy ($H_{n,t}$), an index of how broadly dwell time was distributed across areas of interest:

\begin{equation}
H_{n,t} =\gamma_0+\gamma_{\text{Attention}}\text{LatAttention}_{n,t} + \varepsilon_{n,t},
\end{equation}

where the measurement intercept and loading were fixed at 0 and 1 for identification. Latent attention was predicted by response time, normalized trial, and separate pre- and post-disruption intercepts and slopes. The joint model therefore tested whether current attributes and the previous response changed their influence as visual search narrowed.

Gaze entropy was calculated using Shannon's entropy formula:

\begin{equation}
    H_{nt}=
    -\sum_{k=1}^{K} p_{n,t,a}\log\left(p_{n,t,a}\right),
\label{eq:shannon_entropy}
\end{equation}

where $p_{n,t,a}$ is the proportion of participant $n$'s dwell time allocated to area of interest $a\in \mathcal{A}$ on trial $t$:

\begin{equation}
    p_{n,t,a}=\frac{d_{n,t,a}}{\sum_{a'\in \mathcal{A}}{d_{n,t,a'}}}.
\end{equation}

Conditional on the latent attention, the choice probability is:

\begin{equation} 
\begin{split}
P^C_{n,t,i}=&P(y_{n,t,i}=1|\text{LatAttention}_{n,t},\beta,\gamma) \\
=&\frac{\exp(V(X_{n,t,i}, \beta_{n,t})
+ \beta_{\text{Inertia},n,t} I_{n,t,i})} {\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}}\exp(V(X_{n,t,j}, \beta_{n,t})
+ \beta_{\text{Inertia},n,t} I_{n,t,j})}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}


The measurement equation contribution is:

\begin{equation} 
P^{H}_{n,t,i} = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{\sigma_H}f\left(H_{n,t}\mid \text{LatAttention}_{n,t,i}\right), & H_{n,t}\text{ is observed},\\ 1, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases} 
\end{equation}

with $f$ the standard normal density function. Then, the joint trial-level likelihood is:

\begin{equation} 
\begin{split}
P_{n,t,i}=&P(y_{n,t,i},H_{n,t}|\text{LatAttention}_{n,t},\beta,\gamma)\\
    =&P^{H}_{n,t,i}\prod_{i\in \mathcal{C}} (P^{C}_{n,t,i})^{y_{n,t,i}}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

The full likelihood is:

\begin{equation} 
\begin{split}
L=&\prod_{n=1}^N \prod_{t=1}^T P^{H}_{n,t,i}\prod_{i\in \mathcal{C}} (P^{C}_{n,t,i})^{y_{n,t,i}}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

Because latent attention is unobserved, the random terms were integrated by maximum simulated likelihood using 1,000 intra-individual Halton draws. The model was estimated in Apollo \citep{hess2019apollo} using R 4.4.0 \citep{Rcore}. Laboratory and online data were estimated jointly. Gaze entropy contributed to the measurement equation for laboratory participants; for online participants, the measurement contribution was set to $P^H_{n,t,i}=1$, so their choices contributed to the choice component of the likelihood. Estimation was performed at the National Laboratory for High Performance Computing in the Faculty of Physical and Mathematical Science at the University of Chile.

The behavioral analysis is presented first because it provides the critical test of habitual control in the present task. Overtraining increased staying when current utility favored switching and reduced staying when it favored remaining. At the same time, gaze narrowed into a stereotyped spatial routine. Disrupting the learned layout restored both attentional breadth and value-sensitive staying, while relative preferences remained stable throughout. As shown below, the opposing conditional changes were not evident in the raw probability of repeating a choice.

\subsection{Overtraining reduces control by current value}

Habitual control was defined as staying with the previously chosen alternative on trials where that alternative was no longer the best available ($\delta_{nt}=1$). This is the choice analogue of the devaluation criterion: the response persists although current value does not support it. On trials where the previous alternative remained best ($\delta_{nt}=0$), staying is the response favored by habitual and goal-directed control alike, so an individual repetition there cannot be attributed to either. Figure~\ref{fig:value-staying} therefore shows the habit criterion alone, across early training, late training, and the trials immediately following the disruption. The complementary trials are retained in the analyses below, where the two conditions change in opposite directions and are informative in their own right.

We tested this formally with a beta regression, appropriate because the probability of staying is bounded between 0 and 1. The model predicted $P^{\mathrm{stay}}_{nt}$ from trial ($t$), the manipulation ($M_{nt}=1$ for $t>100$), the diagnostic indicator ($\delta_{nt}$), and all of their interactions, with a participant-specific random intercept (Table~\ref{tab:beta-stay}).

Participants were far less likely to stay when the previous alternative was no longer best ($\beta_{\delta}=-2.813$, $p<.001$), confirming that choice tracked current utility. Across training, however, this control weakened: staying on diagnostic trials rose steadily ($\beta_{t \times \delta}=0.0067$, $p<.001$), while staying on non-diagnostic trials fell ($\beta_{t}=-0.004$, $p<.001$). Because the two move in opposite directions, the overall rate of repetition barely changed while the control of choice by current value was progressively lost.

The disruption reversed the diagnostic effect: value-inconsistent staying fell sharply at trial 101 ($\beta_{M \times \delta}=-0.454$, $p=.032$). Expressed as fitted probabilities, staying on diagnostic trials climbed from .157 at the start of training to .195 by trial 100, and dropped to .165 immediately after the layout changed, returning to within .01 of where it began.

Neither three-way interaction approached significance ($p=.941$ and $p=.740$), and this is informative rather than merely null. The disruption changed the level of habitual control without changing the rate at which it accrued: after trial 101 the diagnostic probability began climbing again at essentially the training slope, reaching .181 by trial 150. The stable layout had been dismantled, but habit immediately began to rebuild in the new one at the same pace as before. The manipulation reset how much habit governed choice, not how quickly habit forms.

These opposing movements explain why the habit is not apparent in marginal repetition. Overtraining did not simply make every response more repetitive. It specifically reduced how strongly current value determined whether participants stayed or switched.

\subsection{Attention narrows during training}

Gaze entropy declined steadily across the stable phase ($r=-.292$, $p<.01$): participants scanned the display in an increasingly narrow pattern as training progressed. The manipulation interrupted this. Entropy rose abruptly at trial 101, and across the disrupted phase it no longer declined with trial number in any meaningful way ($r=-.0066$, $p<.01$). Visual search therefore not only broadened at the moment the layout changed, it stopped renarrowing thereafter, consistent with a display that had ceased to be predictable.

Because the size of such a difference depends on how wide a window is taken around the disruption, we repeated the comparison for every window from 1 to 50 trials on each side (Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity}A). The increase in entropy remained significant across the whole range, so the effect is not an artifact of one arbitrary choice of window. Over a longer horizon attention would presumably renarrow as the new arrangement itself became familiar.

\subsection{Spatial fixation habits form and break}

The narrowing of attention took a specific spatial form. The share of fixation time on the most-attended area of interest rose steadily from around 19\% at trial 1 to 25\% by trial 100 (Figure~\ref{fig:results}C). The probability of returning to the same area on consecutive trials rose from 16\% to 27\% over the same period (Figure~\ref{fig:results}D). Participants were not just attending to fewer attributes in general; they were settling into a fixed spatial routine. When the context changed at trial 101, both patterns reversed immediately. The probability of fixating the same area dropped, and fixation time redistributed across the display.

Comparing the 20 trials on either side of the disruption, the dominant area of interest received between 1.73\% and 3.36\% less of the trial's viewing time afterwards ($p=.05$); comparing only the last trial before with the first trial after, the difference reached 6\%. These magnitudes are substantial relative to the display. With 19 areas of interest, perfectly even viewing would allocate 5.26\% to each, so a shift of 3\% to 6\% represents a large fraction of the whole range over which this measure can move. As with entropy, the difference remained negative and significant for every window from 1 to 50 trials on each side (Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity}B).

\subsection{Raw repetition conceals the transfer of control}

The raw probability of repeating the same choice showed no significant trend during training ($r=-.033$, $p=.744$) or after the manipulation ($r=-.138$, $p=.34$). Figure~\ref{fig:value-staying} shows why. During training, participants repeated less when repetition was value consistent and more when it was value inconsistent. Aggregating across these conditions caused the two changes to cancel.

\subsection{Longer trials are more exploratory}

Across all trials and participants, longer response times were associated with higher gaze entropy. Trials completed quickly involved a narrower visual search. As participants settled into a spatial routine, they also chose faster. Response time is therefore a behavioral correlate of attentional breadth, and the steep RT decline during training (Figure~\ref{fig:results}B) mirrors the entropy decline.

\subsection{Response times replicate online}

Mean response times showed the same trajectory in both the laboratory and online samples: a steep decline during training and a partial recovery after the context change (Figure~\ref{fig:results}B). Comparing the 20 trials on either side of the disruption, responses immediately afterwards were slower by 1.18 to 2.89~s in the laboratory sample and by 0.69 to 2.46~s in the online sample ($p=.05$). The two samples therefore agree both in shape and in magnitude, even though only the laboratory participants were eye-tracked. This confirms that the effect is not specific to the laboratory setting.

The slowing was concentrated around the disruption itself. Repeating the comparison across windows of 1 to 50 trials, the difference was largest for the narrowest windows and vanished as the window widened (Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity}C), so response times returned to their previous level once participants had learned the new arrangement. This is the expected signature of a transient return to controlled processing rather than a lasting change in how long decisions took.

\subsection{The structural model reveals what choices hide}

The structural model estimated what the choice data could not show. Every parameter changed in magnitude across trials. To place them on a common scale, we divided each parameter's estimate at trial $t$ by its estimate on the first trial, which expresses each coefficient as a proportion of its starting value and makes attributes measured in different units directly comparable.

Expressed this way, the attribute parameters and the inertia parameter moved in opposite directions during training (Figure~\ref{fig:value-staying}A). All four attribute parameters declined with trial number, and they declined at very nearly the same rate: the correlations were $-.568$ for CO\textsubscript{2} and comfort and $-.567$ for cost and time, all $p<.001$ (Table~\ref{tab:param-corr}). Inertia rose over the same trials at a comparable rate, $r=.542$, $p<.001$.

That the four attribute parameters declined at a common rate is what keeps relative preferences fixed. Willingness-to-pay is the ratio of each attribute parameter to the cost parameter, so a proportional decline in numerator and denominator leaves the ratio unchanged. Willingness-to-pay for travel time, CO\textsubscript{2}, and comfort accordingly remained stable throughout training and did not shift at the disruption. This combination---falling attribute parameters, rising inertia, and flat willingness-to-pay---is the signature of habitual control identified by this design: participants did not come to want different things, but current attribute information became progressively less able to determine what they chose, while the previous response became more able to.

After the disruption this time dependence largely disappeared. Every correlation fell by roughly a factor of seven, to between $-.086$ and $-.087$ for the attribute parameters and $.082$ for inertia. The parameters stopped drifting with trial number, indicating that the manipulation not only reversed the direction of the inertia effect but arrested the progressive reweighting that had been underway throughout training.

\section{Discussion}

The present study provides evidence that extended training in a stable environment produces habit-like control during human choice. Using a DCE in which the attributes changed on every trial, it was observed that participants became more likely to repeat a previous choice when its current utility no longer supported it. At the same time, they became less likely to stay when the previous choice remained the best alternative. The disruption of the learned layout reversed both effects. More generally, these results show that the classic value criterion for habitual responding can be translated into an ongoing multiattribute choice task.

Importantly, the effect was not evident in the raw probability of repeating a choice. This measure remained stable because it combined two different events: staying when current utility supported staying and staying when it supported switching. These probabilities moved in opposite directions during training and after the layout manipulation. The absence of a trend in raw repetition does not therefore imply that the process controlling choice remained constant. Rather, overtraining changed the conditions under which previous choices were repeated.

These data are readily anticipated by theories in which environmental uncertainty determines whether goal-directed or habitual systems control behavior \citep{daw2005, lee2014}. For 100 trials, participants encountered the same visual organization and could rely increasingly on the learned context and their previous responses. At trial 101, the layout changed, but the alternatives, their attributes, and the economic structure of the task did not. The immediate return of value-sensitive choice is consistent with the idea that uncertainty made previous responses less reliable and brought current information back into control. Although no formal arbitration model was fitted, this account explains why stability, rather than repetition in isolation, is important for the present effect.

Attention provides a possible mechanism for this transition. During the stable phase, visual search became narrower and more stereotyped: participants returned to the same locations, consulted labels less, and responded faster. When the layout was disrupted, visual search broadened at the same time that value sensitivity returned. This result accords with animal data linking habitual responding to reduced attention to action consequences \citep{thrailkill2018, bouton2020, bouton2024}. Response time alone could not establish this point because fast decisions can result either from efficient evaluation or from failing to evaluate current information. The eye movements show that the content of visual search changed with training. They do not, however, establish that attention causally mediated the behavioral effect, as spatial stability rather than attention itself was manipulated.

The structural model gives a similar description of the choice process. Attribute coefficients declined proportionally during training, so the WTP ratios remained stable, whereas previous-choice inertia acquired more influence. In other words, participants did not come to value time, cost, comfort, or emissions differently; instead, these attributes became less effective in determining choice. A general loss of engagement could also reduce the magnitude of the attribute coefficients, but would not readily explain the increase in value-inconsistent staying, the opposite movement of inertia, or the recovery of value sensitivity after disruption. The agreement between the conditional probabilities and the structural model is therefore important.

One novel aspect of the present design is the absence of outcome feedback. In a conventional instrumental procedure, devaluation asks whether responding persists after the value of an obtained outcome changes. No outcome was obtained here: the displayed attributes provided all the information about the current value of each alternative. Habitual control was therefore defined as repeating a previous choice when those values supported another response. This is a functional analogue of outcome insensitivity, and is consistent with Guthrie's contiguity account and with recent computational models in which repetition can strengthen responses without value reinforcement \citep{guthrie1935, millerHabits2019}.

Related evidence comes from the unrewarded-habit task. \citet{nebe2024} manipulated the frequency of instructed responses to neutral stimuli and found a small bias in a subsequent free-choice test. The present task differs in two important respects. The choices were freely generated, and the value of the alternatives changed continuously during training. Thus, the results do not merely show that frequent responding can bias a later choice, but that repetition can reduce control by current value during the task in which the habit develops.

Previous DCE studies have shown that incomplete attribute attendance can affect preference estimates \citep{yegoryan2020}, and that self-reported habits and previous choices can predict subsequent attention and choice \citep{blake2020}. However, these studies did not investigate whether overtraining produced a habit during the experiment. Habit was measured by self-report before the task, or previous choice was treated as a learning effect without testing whether it opposed current utility. The present analysis provides such a test: repetition is habit-like only when the current attributes favor a different alternative.

Several limitations should be noted. The eye-tracking sample was modest and the habit analysis was not preregistered. In addition, the stable phase always preceded the disrupted phase, and therefore the immediate recovery at trial 101 may include an effect of novelty. Participants' own reports confirm that the change was noticed and was experienced as surprising, and that the row colors were the cue by which it was detected. This is what the present account requires, since a disruption that went unnoticed could not recapture attention, but it does mean that the recovery cannot be separated from the surprise that accompanied it. Novelty cannot, however, explain the gradual increase in value-inconsistent staying during stable training, which developed while the environment was entirely unchanged. Finally, option identities remained in fixed columns while attribute rows were shuffled. The experiment shows that disrupting the learned spatial environment reduced habit-like choice, but it cannot fully separate an option habit from a motor or spatial response habit. A direct manipulation of attention will also be necessary to establish whether attentional narrowing is required for the behavioral effect.

In summary, extended choice in a stable DCE narrowed attention and made previous responses more likely to persist when current utility supported switching. This change occurred while relative preferences remained stable and in the absence of outcome feedback. Disrupting the learned environment restored broader attention and value-sensitive choice. The present results suggest that stable repetition may be sufficient for habit control in humans.
